2. Analysis of each area

## 1) Facial recognition systems misidentifying minorities

### Summary of the case/example
Facial recognition technology has repeatedly shown bias against people from ethnic minority groups, particularly women with darker skin. Research such as the Gender Shades project demonstrated that some systems had much higher error rates for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. In real-world contexts, this has led to false matches, wrongful accusations, and discriminatory surveillance in public spaces, policing, and border control.

### Causes of the EDI failure
The failure is usually rooted in biased data. Many facial recognition datasets are dominated by images of lighter-skinned men, so the system learns patterns that perform poorly on other groups. In addition, labels and annotations may be inconsistent, and there may be little testing on diverse populations before deployment. A lack of oversight and accountability also allows systems to be used in sensitive contexts without adequate fairness checks.

### Impact on individuals, organisations and society
For individuals, the impact can include humiliation, loss of freedom, and damaged reputations. For organisations, biased systems can lead to legal challenges, reputational damage, and poor trust from customers and communities. At a societal level, discriminatory technology can reinforce stereotypes, increase fear of institutions, and widen existing inequalities, especially where marginalised groups are over-surveilled or misidentified.

### Lessons that can be learned
The main lesson is that technology is not neutral: if the data and testing process are biased, the system will be biased. Organisations must collect representative datasets, test systems across diverse demographics, and evaluate outcomes before deployment. Human oversight and clear accountability are essential, particularly in high-risk areas such as policing and access control.

## 2) Discriminatory hiring algorithms

### Summary of the case/example
A well-known example is Amazon’s recruiting tool, which was designed to help rank applicants. The system was later found to disadvantage women because it had been trained on historical hiring data that reflected a male-dominated workforce. As a result, the algorithm learned to prefer patterns associated with male applicants and downgraded resumes that included terms or experiences more common among women.

### Causes of the EDI failure
This case shows how biased historical data can be encoded into automated decisions. If past hiring practices were unfair, an algorithm trained on that data may replicate and scale those inequities. There was also a lack of bias auditing, insufficient human review, and limited awareness of how proxy variables (such as school names or wording in CVs) can stand in for protected characteristics.

### Impact on individuals, organisations and society
Applicants were denied fair opportunities, which affected their income, career progression, and confidence. Organisations missed out on diverse talent, which can reduce innovation and limit the range of ideas in technical teams. At a societal level, discriminatory hiring systems preserve unequal access to high-value jobs and reinforce barriers for underrepresented groups.

### Lessons that can be learned
Algorithmic recruitment should not be treated as objective or infallible. Employers need to audit their systems for fairness, monitor outcomes across different groups, and ensure human decision-makers remain accountable. Recruitment tools should be designed with inclusive criteria, not simply on historical success patterns that may reflect existing discrimination.

## 3) Under-representation in cybersecurity leadership roles

### Summary of the case/example
Cybersecurity continues to struggle with unequal representation in leadership. Women and many ethnic minority groups are often well represented in entry-level and technical roles, but less visible in senior positions such as Chief Information Security Officer (CISO), security managers, or board-level technology leadership. This pattern reflects a wider lack of inclusion within the sector and in its pathways to promotion.

### Causes of the EDI failure
There are several systematic causes. Some organisations rely on narrow ideas of “culture fit,” which can favour candidates who resemble current leaders rather than those with the strongest capability. There may also be less access to mentoring, sponsorship, and networking opportunities, especially for underrepresented groups. In some cases, promotion criteria are unclear, and bias in performance evaluation reduces the chances of advancement.

### Impact on individuals, organisations and society
For individuals, under-representation can lead to exclusion, isolation, and limited career progression. For organisations, the lack of diversity in leadership can produce weaker decision-making, poorer understanding of risks, and less trust among employees and customers. In society, underrepresented leadership means that security systems and policies may be designed without the needs and perspectives of a broad range of users.

### Lessons that can be learned
The sector must address fairness at every stage of career progression, not only in recruitment. Inclusive promotion practices, transparent criteria, mentoring programmes, and strong accountability for leadership decisions can help create healthier workplace cultures and more effective organisations. Diverse leadership improves problem-solving and helps ensure that security strategies reflect the needs of all communities.

3. Reflection

### Why addressing EDI matters in cybersecurity and technology
EDI matters because cybersecurity is not only a technical challenge; it is also a human and social challenge. Security systems affect people’s rights, safety, opportunities, and trust in institutions. If a system is built by a narrow and unrepresentative group, it may overlook risks faced by minority communities or embed bias into tools that are meant to protect people. In cybersecurity, diverse teams are better placed to identify vulnerabilities, challenge assumptions, and create systems that are fair, effective, and trustworthy.

### Practical steps that could be taken to avoid such failures in the future
Organisations and technology teams can take several practical actions:

- Use diverse and representative datasets when training systems such as facial recognition or AI tools.
- Carry out regular bias audits and fairness testing before and after deployment.
- Include human oversight in high-risk decisions and avoid fully automated decisions that affect people’s rights.
- Improve recruitment and promotion practices to reduce discrimination and expand access to leadership roles.
- Build inclusive workplace cultures through mentoring, sponsorship, and accountability for equitable progression.
- Train staff on EDI issues, bias awareness, and the ethical impacts of technology.
- Set clear governance standards and ensure leaders are responsible for ethical outcomes.
- Involve affected communities in the design and evaluation of systems to identify risks that internal teams may miss.

Overall, EDI failures in technology are not just moral problems; they are operational and security risks. Addressing EDI is essential to creating safer, fairer, and more effective systems for everyone.
