Question 1
You are part of the cybersecurity team at a mid-sized financial services company. Recently, several employees reported suspicious emails that appeared to be from senior management, asking them to click on links and approve urgent payments.

Task

1. Identify risks 

List two potential risks to the company if the phishing attack succeeds (think: financial, operational, legal or reputational).
Which risks would be most critical to address first, and why?
2. Apply the framework 

Using what you have learned from the reading:

explain how the framework could help your team assess vulnerability (e.g. the likelihood of being targeted even after controls are in place)
identify one way that explainable AI could support transparency and trust in your analysis (e.g. showing why a URL was flagged as phishing).
3. Recommend actions 

Suggest two strategies the company could adopt to mitigate phishing risk. You may consider:

technical controls (e.g. email filtering, MFA, URL blocking)

administrative controls (e.g. staff training, phishing simulations)

risk transfer options (e.g. cyber-insurance).

Justify your choices by linking them to the risks identified in 1. Identify risks.

Reflection

In a short paragraph, answer the following question.

How does using explainable AI in phishing risk management add value compared to traditional black-box detection methods?

1. Identify risks

Two major risks if the phishing attack succeeds are:

- Financial loss: fraudulent payment approvals could result in direct monetary loss, plus recovery costs and possible compensation claims.
- Operational and legal risk: compromised employee accounts or systems could disrupt internal processes, expose customer data, and trigger regulatory breaches or lawsuits.

The most critical risk to address first is financial loss because it can happen immediately and be very difficult to reverse once funds leave the company. Operational and legal consequences are also serious, but they often follow the initial compromise and may be more visible after the immediate financial harm has already occurred.

2. Apply the framework

The framework helps the team assess likely attack paths, vulnerabilities, and residual risk after controls are in place. Even with email filtering and access controls, the company remains vulnerable because phishing depends on human behaviour and social engineering. The framework would help evaluate the chance of employees being targeted, the effectiveness of current safeguards, and the likelihood that a spoofed email from a senior manager could still convince someone to act quickly. It also highlights that risk is not only technical; it depends on awareness, process discipline, and decision-making under pressure.

Explainable AI can support transparency and trust by showing which features led to a phishing alert. For example, it could explain that the URL used a lookalike domain, a suspicious redirect pattern, or a mismatch with the sender’s expected branding. This makes the analysis more understandable to cybersecurity staff and senior managers, increasing confidence that the detection is valid and helping teams explain decisions to auditors or stakeholders.

3. Recommend actions

Two effective strategies are:

- Technical controls: strengthen email security using advanced filtering, URL reputation checks, domain authentication (SPF/DKIM/DMARC), and mandatory MFA for payment systems. Require a second verification step before approving high-value or urgent transfers. These controls directly reduce the chance of malicious messages reaching employees and prevent a fraudulent payment from being approved quickly.
- Administrative controls: deliver regular phishing awareness training and run simulated phishing exercises. Create a policy that urgent payment requests must be confirmed through a second channel, such as a phone call to a trusted contact or a formal approval workflow. This reduces the human vulnerability that phishing attacks exploit and helps prevent impersonation attempts from senior management accounts.

These choices directly address the risks identified earlier. Technical controls reduce the risk of financial loss and limit operational disruption, while staff training and verification procedures reduce the chance that employees will be manipulated into authorising fraudulent payments. Cyber insurance could also be considered as a risk transfer option, but it should complement rather than replace prevention controls because it does not stop the attack or the immediate business damage.

Reflection

Explainable AI adds value because it makes phishing detection more transparent, trustworthy, and actionable than traditional black-box systems. Instead of simply labelling a message as suspicious, explainable AI can show why it was flagged, such as a spoofed domain, malicious URL, or urgency-based manipulation tactics. This helps analysts understand the alert, respond appropriately, and communicate decisions to managers or regulators. It also improves confidence in the system, supports better training and policy decisions, and strengthens overall phishing risk management compared with methods that provide little or no explanation for their decisions.
